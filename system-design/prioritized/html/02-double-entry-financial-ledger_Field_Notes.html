1. Problem & Requirements

Design a financial ledger that reliably records money movement and maintains correct account balances.

Functional requirements

  • Record financial transactions using debit and credit entries.
  • Maintain accurate balances.
  • Support refunds, reversals, and adjustments.
  • Prevent duplicate postings.
  • Provide transaction history and audit records.

Non-functional requirements

  • Strong consistency: Financial entries must be correct.
  • Atomicity: All entries of a transaction succeed or fail together.
  • Immutability: Posted financial records cannot be silently modified.
  • High availability and durability: No loss of acknowledged financial postings.
  • Auditability: Every change must be traceable.

Key assumption: We are designing the authoritative ledger for a financial product, not just a transaction-history API.

2. Understand Double-Entry Accounting

Every financial transaction records both a debit and a credit.

Example: A $100 credit card purchase clears (simplified issuer-accounting perspective):

Account Debit Credit
Cardholder Receivable (Asset) $100 —
Merchant Settlement Payable (Liability) — $100
Total $100 $100

Core invariant: Sum(debits) = Sum(credits) for each posted journal transaction, separately for each currency.

The actual accounting entries depend on the issuer, network, settlement model, and applicable accounting rules. Debit and credit do not simply mean money leaving or entering a bank account; their effect depends on account type.

Why not simply update a balance? A balance-only database may show the current amount without reliably preserving why it changed. A double-entry ledger stores the accounting effect and allows balances to be reconstructed and checked.

Why these components?

  • Ledger Service: Centralizes accounting rules and posting.
  • Transactional DB: Commits all related financial entries together.
  • Balance table: Enables fast balance lookups.
  • Idempotency record: Prevents the same business event from posting twice.
  • Outbox and event broker: Distribute committed financial events to downstream systems.

The journal is the accounting source of truth; reporting and analytics are derived views.

Decision 1 — Why ACID Transactions?

BEGIN TRANSACTION
  Debit Receivable      $100
  Credit Payable        $100
  Update account balances
  Save idempotency record
  Save outbox event
COMMIT

If one required operation fails, the whole transaction rolls back. A debit without its matching credit must never commit.

Decision 2 — Why Immutable Journal Entries?

If a $100 posting is wrong, do not overwrite it with $80. Preserve the original and create reversing and corrected journal entries.

Why: Auditability, traceability, reconciliation, and the ability to reconstruct prior financial states.

Concept — append-only: Posted accounting entries are not changed or deleted; corrections are additional entries.

Decision 3 — How Do We Read Balances Quickly?

Option A: Calculate balances from all journal entries. This is reliable but may become expensive for large accounts.

Option B: Maintain a balance table together with each journal posting.

Ledger Posting
      |
      +--> Append journal entries
      |
      +--> Update account balances
      |
      v
Commit together

Choice: Option B. The journal remains the source of truth; balances are transactionally maintained projections that are independently checked against it.

Decision 4 — How Do We Prevent Duplicate Postings?

A clearing event can arrive more than once. Give the logical business posting a stable, properly scoped unique identity.

The database unique constraint is the final protection against simultaneous duplicates.

Remember: Exactly-once business effect comes from durable idempotency, not a guarantee that the message is delivered only once.

Decision 5 — How Do We Handle Concurrency?

A prepaid account with $100 must not approve two simultaneous $80 withdrawals. Double-entry accounting by itself does not prevent overdrafts.

Use an atomic balance condition or appropriate row locks within the posting transaction. This enforces spending rules as well as balanced accounting.

Decision 6 — How Do We Scale?

First scale stateless API workers and optimize queries, indexes, connection pools, and database capacity.

Shard only when necessary. A journal entry may move value between accounts, so naive account-ID sharding can split one posting across databases. Prefer keeping related accounts in one accounting partition or using a database with the required cross-partition atomic transactions.

Critical: Balance checks, journal entries, and authoritative balance updates must be coordinated within one transaction where they enforce financial invariants.

For multi-currency transactions, define FX conversion and dedicated balancing/clearing accounts. A USD debit and EUR credit do not directly balance numerically.

6. Failure Handling, Scalability & Security

Scenario Design response Why
Failure after first journal entry Roll back entire transaction Avoid partial posting.
Duplicate request Durable idempotency Avoid double posting.
Response lost after commit Return stored result on retry Preserve one business effect.
Broker unavailable Transactional outbox Retain event for later delivery.
Incorrect posting New reversal/correction entries Preserve the audit history.
Concurrent withdrawals Atomic balance enforcement Prevent invalid negative balances.
Database or region failure Durable replication and fenced failover Avoid lost/conflicting writes.
Balance mismatch Reconcile against journal and external sources Detect discrepancies.

Security and operations

  • Encrypt sensitive data and restrict posting permissions.
  • Separate ledger writers from reporting readers.
  • Preserve tamper-evident audit trails.
  • Test backups, restoration, and reconciliation.
  • Define recovery point and recovery time objectives.
  • Avoid independently writable regional replicas without correct coordination.

Concept — reconciliation: Comparing financial records or balances with another authoritative source to detect missing, duplicated, incorrect, or unmatched transactions.

Important: A balanced journal can still be economically wrong. Equal debit and credit totals are necessary, not sufficient, for correctness.

7. Key Points to Remember

  1. Double-entry: For each posted journal, total debits equal total credits.
  2. ACID: Entries and authoritative balance updates commit together.
  3. Immutability: Do not rewrite posted entries; reverse and correct them.
  4. Idempotency: Retries must not create duplicate financial postings.
  5. Source of truth: The journal is authoritative; materialized balances improve performance and must be reconciled.

8. Interview Closing Summary

I would design the financial ledger using double-entry accounting backed by a transactional database. Every posting has balanced debit and credit entries committed atomically with balance updates. Posted entries are immutable, and corrections use reversing entries. Durable idempotency prevents duplicate postings. I would maintain materialized balances for fast reads and use a transactional outbox for downstream events. For scale, I would partition carefully without breaking journal atomicity. Reconciliation, strict access controls, and tested recovery protect financial correctness and auditability.
